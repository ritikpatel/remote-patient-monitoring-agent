### The minority label, and why conditional training is not optional
| paradigm       |   synthetic_positive_rate |   real_positive_rate |
|:---------------|--------------------------:|---------------------:|
| conditional    |                    0.0401 |               0.0401 |
| nonconditional |                    0.0309 |               0.0401 |

The nonconditional generator -- the paradigm the tutorial demonstrates -- averages **0.0309** positives against a real rate of **0.0401**, and the average hides the real problem. Early in training it collapses the class almost entirely: **0.0041** at epoch 50, roughly 10x under-represented, which leaves too few positives to fit a classifier on at all. Even at its best it never reliably holds the rate, because nothing in the objective requires it to.

This is the failure the tutorial predicts for low-prevalence concepts: generative models "cannot accurately capture the statistical properties of these variables ... due to the limited observations in the data set". At a 4% base rate in 2,979 rows the outcome *is* a low-prevalence concept.

The conditional paradigm holds the rate exactly, because the label is an input rather than something the generator has to learn to emit. That is the tutorial's own remedy -- conditional training "enables the control over the categories of the generated data in terms of the label variables" -- and stage 2 uses it throughout.

### Privacy: the finding that does not transfer from the paper

| scenario                                     |   membership F1 |
|:---------------------------------------------|----------------:|
| unrelated synthetic set (chance floor)       |          0.5132 |
| EMR-WGAN, final checkpoint (mean of 10 runs) |          0.8407 |
| releasing the real training data itself      |          1      |

The generator sits **67% of the way** from chance to the risk of simply publishing the real records. For comparison, the paper's five synthetic data sets score 0.29-0.31 against a real-data baseline of 0.91 -- their synthetic data substantially reduces disclosure risk, and this cohort's does not.

Risk also **rises** monotonically with training length (0.757 at epoch 50 to 0.841 at epoch 300), which is the signature of memorisation rather than generalisation. This is the expected consequence of the scale gap: the paper fits 181,294 patients, this fits roughly 2,000 rows from 65 subjects, and a network with this much capacity on that little data reproduces its training set rather than the distribution behind it.


**The confound is ruled out.** A membership figure this high could mean the attack is reading a fixed difference between the two subject groups rather than anything the generator memorised, so `privacy_control.py` refits the generator on the *held-out* group and re-runs the identical attack against the identical targets:

| generator fitted on   |   attack scores A as members |
|:----------------------|-----------------------------:|
| A                     |                       0.8283 |
| B                     |                       0.1646 |

Group A is identified as the training set at **0.828** by a generator that saw A, and at **0.165** by one that saw B instead -- a swing of +0.664 on the same targets. The verdict follows the generator's training set, which is memorisation and not a cohort artefact.

**Consequence: the synthetic cohort generated here must not be treated as de-identified and must not be shared outside the project.** That is the one use the tutorial's method exists to support, and it is the use this cohort cannot safely make of it.

## 3. Whole synthetic patients, class-balanced

Section 2 generates patient-*hours*: rows with no identity, no trajectory, and no way to raise the count of positive **subjects** -- which is the quantity [`reliability.py`](../evaluation/reliability.py) identifies as binding, at 49 against the ~725 a 0.10-wide AUPRC interval would need. The fair objection is that patient-hours are not patients.

[`patients.py`](patients.py) removes the objection. Each synthetic patient gets a length of stay, an hourly trajectory across nine vitals, static attributes, an event time if it deteriorates, and its own subject id, generated 50/50 by class. Derived features are **recomputed** from the generated trajectory by the same `engineer.add_rolling_features` the real pipeline uses, so a synthetic patient's `hr_4h_std` describes its own heart rate rather than being an independently generated column.

Baseline: **39 positive subjects** per training fold.

|   synthetic patients per real one |   positive subjects |   mean AUPRC |   delta vs real-only | paired wins   | meets 15/20 bar   |
|----------------------------------:|--------------------:|-------------:|---------------------:|:--------------|:------------------|
|                                 1 |                  89 |       0.4927 |              -0.01   | 9/20          | no                |
|                                 3 |                 189 |       0.4956 |              -0.0072 | 7/20          | no                |
|                                10 |                 539 |       0.469  |              -0.0337 | 9/20          | no                |

**The positive-subject count is no longer the constraint, and nothing improves.** The largest arm manufactures **539 positive subjects** -- 14x the real count -- and AUPRC is -0.0337 against the real-only baseline, winning 9/20 paired comparisons. No arm clears the 15/20 bar; the best is 9/20, which is a coin flip.

The confidence interval does not move either: 0.573 wide on the real-only arm against 0.593 at the largest synthetic multiplier. That is the *correct* behaviour and worth stating plainly, because it is easy to misread as a failure. The interval is measured on real held-out patients, and synthetic patients never enter a test fold, so nothing about the training side can narrow it. An interval that *did* tighten here would be the warning sign -- it would mean synthetic rows had reached the evaluation, which is exactly the manufactured precision [`synthetic_ceiling_report.md`](../evaluation/synthetic_ceiling_report.md) measures. What the reliability analysis asks for is ~725 positive subjects *observed*, which is a statement about how many real patients the cohort contains, and no generator changes that number.

This is the cleanest statement of the result in the whole report. A synthetic positive subject is not a positive subject. The count went up more than tenfold and bought nothing, because all of it was derived from the same 39 real positive subjects the generator was fitted to -- and no amount of resampling an estimate tells you about the patients that estimate never saw.

## 4. What this establishes

**The generator works.** Dimension-wise distance reaches 1.44, inside the 0.52-1.56 band the paper reports for its own five runs on 181,294 patients, and a model trained purely on synthetic rows scores AUROC 0.800 against the real model's 0.827 on the same held-out patients. This is not a broken implementation producing a null result.

**It still does not buy the thing it was reached for.** Not one augmented arm beats the real-only baseline: the best of six is match, k=3 at -0.0006 AUPRC, winning 10 of 20 paired comparisons against a bar of 15. The largest synthetic multiplier is the *worst* arm, which is the direction to expect if synthetic rows add distribution shift rather than information. Section 3 makes the same point at the patient level, where the positive-subject count rises more than tenfold and AUPRC still does not.

This settles the question [`synthetic_ceiling_report.md`](../evaluation/synthetic_ceiling_report.md) left open. That report measured a resampling generator and could only reach 100% or 0% fidelity, never the middle, so it could not rule out that a *real* generator at intermediate fidelity would behave differently. One does now exist, it sits in that band, and it behaves the same way. The constraint was never fidelity. A generator fitted on 74 training subjects is an estimate of a distribution, and sampling an estimate does not add information about the population behind it -- which is why the honest lever on this project's confidence interval remains more real patients (`warehouse/fetch_mimic4.py`), not more rows.

**The privacy result is the one that changes what may be done with the output.** The tutorial's method exists to make data shareable; on this cohort it produces records that an adversary can trace back to their training subjects at two-thirds of the risk of publishing the real data outright. The synthetic cohort is therefore usable *inside* the project for the purposes [`README.md`](README.md) lists, and is not publishable.
